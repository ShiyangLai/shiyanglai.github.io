# Social Superposition

One of Shiyang's long-term intellectual interests is a developing idea he calls "social superposition."

The core intuition is that social actors rarely possess a single, stable, fully realized identity. Instead, individuals simultaneously occupy multiple potential social positions, roles, perspectives, and forms of knowledge.

Which aspect becomes visible depends on context, interaction partners, institutions, and situations.

In this view, apparent social identities are not fixed entities. They are temporary realizations from a larger latent space of possibilities.

This idea is inspired by:

- Social theory
- Identity theory
- Role theory
- Network analysis
- Scientific knowledge production
- AI representation learning
- Neural-network superposition

The goal is not to directly apply quantum mechanics to society. Rather, social superposition is a theoretical lens for understanding how multiple social possibilities coexist before becoming expressed in a specific context.

# Research Work

Shiyang's work can be organized into several connected streams.

## Human-AI Interaction and Decision-Making

In "Biased AI Improves Human Decision-Making But Reduces Trust," Shiyang and collaborators study whether politically biased AI assistants can improve human evaluation of misinformation.

The central idea is counterintuitive: AI bias is usually treated as something to eliminate, but strategically visible bias may sometimes disrupt automation bias and make users think more critically.

The project shows that biased AI assistants can improve decision-making, increase engagement, and reduce evaluative bias, especially when users encounter opposing perspectives. At the same time, these gains come with a trust penalty: users tend to underappreciate biased AI and overcredit neutral systems.

This work reflects one of Shiyang's broader interests: disagreement can be productive when it is institutionally organized well.

## Multi-Agent AI and Semantic Collapse

Shiyang studies what happens when multiple AI systems interact with one another over time.

A central concern in this line of work is semantic collapse: the tendency of interacting LLMs to converge toward narrow, stable semantic regions even when diversity, randomness, or multiple agents are introduced.

This connects directly to his social-theoretical interest in how systems lose possibility. A system may appear diverse at the start, yet repeated interaction can gradually compress it into a much smaller realized space.

For Shiyang, semantic collapse is not only an AI phenomenon. It is also a metaphor and model for how social systems, institutions, and intellectual communities may lose diversity through repeated interaction.

## Societies of Thought

In "Reasoning Models Generate Societies of Thought," Shiyang and collaborators examine why reasoning models perform better on complex tasks.

The key idea is that stronger reasoning may not come only from longer chains of thought, but from internal diversification: reasoning models appear to simulate multiple perspectives, roles, and voices inside their reasoning process.

This project connects directly to Shiyang's broader fascination with collective intelligence. Reasoning may work better when it resembles an organized internal society rather than a single linear voice.

This also resonates with his social superposition idea: cognition contains multiple possible perspectives, and good reasoning may depend on how those perspectives are activated, organized, debated, and reconciled.

## Mechanistic Interpretability and Polysemanticity

In "Signal in the Noise: Polysemantic Interference Transfers and Predicts Cross-Model Influence," Shiyang and collaborators study how polysemantic structures inside language models can generate predictable behavioral effects.

The project examines how features that appear semantically unrelated may interfere with one another inside models, and how such interference can transfer across model families and scales.

For Shiyang, this work is not only about model internals. It is also connected to a deeper question about representation: how can many meanings coexist inside the same representational space, and how do some meanings become expressed in behavior?

This makes mechanistic interpretability another site where his social-theoretical intuition appears: latent structures shape realized action.

## Representational Similarity and Model Interaction

In work on representational similarity and model interactions, Shiyang is interested in how the internal similarity between models may shape how they influence one another.

The broader question is whether interaction dynamics among AI systems can be predicted from the structure of their representations.

This connects to his larger interest in how hidden similarity, difference, and alignment shape visible social behavior.

## LLM Agents and Trust

In "Can Large Language Model Agents Simulate Human Trust Behavior?", Shiyang and collaborators study whether LLM agents can reproduce trust-like behavior in behavioral-economic settings such as trust games.

This work sits at the intersection of social simulation, behavioral economics, and AI agents.

It reflects Shiyang's interest in whether artificial agents can function as models of social behavior, and where such simulations succeed or fail.

## AI Safety and Model Editing

In work on whether editing LLMs can inject harm, Shiyang contributes to questions about model editing, safety, and unintended behavioral side effects.

This connects to his broader skepticism toward simple intervention narratives. Changing an intelligent system in one place may produce unexpected consequences elsewhere.

## Multimodal Misinformation

In work on multimodal large language models and Chinese short-video misinformation, Shiyang and collaborators examine how AI systems respond to misinformation embedded in visual, social, and cognitive-bias cues.

This connects to his broader interest in misinformation, social cues, and the interaction between cognition and media environments.

## Entrepreneurship and Innovation

In "Big Data and the Computational Social Science of Entrepreneurship and Innovation," Shiyang and collaborators discuss how large-scale data and machine-learning models can transform the study of entrepreneurship and innovation.

The work argues that big data and big models can serve both as observatories of innovation systems and as laboratories for virtual experimentation.

This connects to Shiyang's interest in organizations, new ventures, technological change, and the computational study of social and economic systems.
